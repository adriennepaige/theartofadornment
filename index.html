THE RESEARCH

An ongoing investigation into adornment as art, creativity, and cultural expression.

Why adornment?

I've been interested in fashion and beauty for as long as I can remember. Growing up, I loved experimenting with my mom's makeup, doing my friends' hair, and trying different styles. At one point, I wanted to become a fashion designer, and later I studied cosmetology. Over time, my interests expanded to include nails, tattoos, piercings, jewelry, and other ways people decorate their bodies.

What interests me most about adornment is the variety of ways people approach it. Some styles are simple and understated, while others are elaborate, unconventional, or completely different from anything I would personally wear. I'm especially drawn to unusual silhouettes, intricate details, and creative combinations that I wouldn't have thought of myself.

I've realized that appreciating a particular style doesn't necessarily mean wanting to wear it. Sometimes I'm more interested in the creativity, craftsmanship, or thought that went into creating a look. This has made me curious about why certain things catch our attention and how we develop our ideas of what is beautiful.

This project begins with that curiosity. By collecting images, written references, and different examples of adornment, I want to explore the connections between these practices and consider how they can be appreciated as forms of art and creative expression.

What counts as adornment?

Adornment goes beyond the clothing and accessories we wear. It also includes the ways we style, decorate, and alter our appearance. Hair, makeup, nails, tattoos, and piercings are all different forms of adornment, each with their own histories, techniques, and creative possibilities. Alison Goodrum Goodrum, 2016 ↗ approaches dress as a broad subject that includes many of these practices, rather than limiting it to clothing alone.

Although these practices are often treated as separate subjects, they share many similarities. Whether someone is designing a garment, creating an elaborate hairstyle, or working on a tattoo, they are making decisions about shape, color, texture, materials, and composition. Sanda Miller Miller, 2007 ↗ examines the relationship between fashion and art, particularly the idea that clothing can have both practical and artistic value. Her discussion raises questions about how other forms of adornment might also be appreciated as art.

I'm interested in what happens when these practices are considered together rather than individually. While each has its own cultural significance and traditions, looking at them alongside one another might reveal unexpected similarities in how they are created and appreciated.

This broader understanding of adornment is important to my research because it allows me to explore different forms of creativity without limiting the collection to a particular medium, style, or definition of beauty.

Questions I'm asking

1. What influences our ideas of beauty and aesthetic taste, and why are we drawn to certain styles even when they don't reflect our personal preferences?

2. How do fashion imagery and visual culture influence which bodies, styles, and forms of adornment are considered beautiful or worthy of attention?

3. How can dress and adornment be appreciated as forms of creativity and experimentation beyond trends, functionality, or a fixed personal identity?

4. How can collecting and curating different forms of adornment create new visual connections and encourage a broader appreciation of beauty?

Thinking through the research

My research has led me to think more about how our ideas of beauty and personal taste are influenced by the images we encounter. What we consider beautiful is not necessarily something we develop independently. Fashion imagery, cultural expectations, and the things we see repeatedly can all influence what we notice and appreciate.

Elizabeth Wilson's *The Postmodern Chameleon* explores the changing relationship between fashion and identity. Her writing has encouraged me to think about style as something that can change and evolve rather than something that needs to remain consistent.

Brian Moeran's *Proposing Fashion: The Discourse of Glossy Magazines* examines how fashion magazines present and communicate fashion. This relates to my interest in how editorial imagery influences which styles, bodies, and appearances receive attention.

Leah Scolere and Lee Humphreys' *Pinning Design: The Curatorial Labor of Creative Professionals* has also been important to my research. Their discussion of collecting and organizing visual references has helped me think about curation as a creative process rather than simply gathering images.

Together, these readings have encouraged me to look more closely at how beauty is represented, how aesthetic preferences develop, and how curating different forms of adornment might introduce new ways of appreciating them.

The act of collecting

Collecting is an important part of how I develop ideas as a designer. I often begin with images that interest me before I fully understand the relationships between them. As more material is gathered, similarities and patterns begin to appear.
